Dear Dr.~Wohl,

Thank you very much for the opportunity to revise and resubmit our manuscript, \textit{``Stay, React, or Conform? Examining Social Influence Effects on Aesthetic Judgments''} (POETICS-D-26-00490), for consideration in \textit{Poetics}. We are deeply grateful to you and the two anonymous reviewers for the constructive, insightful, and encouraging feedback.

We have carefully considered all comments from the editorial decision and the review reports. In response, we have developed a thorough revision addressing the methodological, conceptual, and empirical points raised. Specifically, our revision:

\end{enumerate}

Below, we provide a point-by-point response to all comments and suggestions from the Editor and both Reviewers.

\section*{Response to Reviewer \#1}

\subsection*{Point 1: Analytical Strategy \& Difference-in-Differences (DiD) Approach}

\begin{reviewercomment}
1. The analytical strategy may not be the most appropriate. To better isolate the treatment effects, the authors may consider applying a difference-in-differences approach, using the no-information condition as the control group and exploiting the pre-treatment measurement. Without such a comparison, the observed changes could potentially be attributed to repeated measurement rather than the information treatment itself. The authors refer to two ``baselines''---the no-information condition and the measurement conducted before treatment---which suggests that a difference-in-differences design may be feasible.
\end{reviewercomment}

\textbf{Status:} \textcolor{forestgreen}{\textbf{[Addressed]}} \\
\textbf{Location in Revised Manuscript:} Section 2.7 (Difference-in-Differences Analytic Strategy), Section 3.1 (Baseline Exposure Drift and Generalized Social Influence), Section 3.2 (Status-Differentiated Social Influence Across Educational Groups), Figure 3, Figure 4, Table 2, Appendix Table A.2, and Appendix Table A.3

\textbf{Response / Actions Taken:}
We want to warmly and wholeheartedly thank the reviewer for their exceptional perceptiveness. Pushing us to adopt the Difference-in-Differences (DiD) framework was by far the most consequential and transformative recommendation we received. It not only rescued the paper from a fundamental inferential trap that we had failed to recognize in our original submission, but it also uncovered striking causal patterns that were previously masked.

\subsubsection*{1. What the Original Analysis Was Doing vs.~What DiD Accomplishes}
In our original submission (\texttt{manuscript.tex}), we evaluated simple pre-to-post change scores within each experimental cell:
\begin{equation*}
H_0: \Delta_{\text{within}} = (\bar{Y}_{k, \text{Trial 2}} - \bar{Y}_{k, \text{Trial 1}}) = 0
\end{equation*}
This one-sample test simply asked: \textit{Did respondents in condition $k$ change their aesthetic rating between Trial 1 and Trial 2?}

As the reviewer brilliantly anticipated, this specification fell directly into the trap of conflating the \textbf{treatment effect} of external social information with the \textbf{mere exposure effect}---the natural upward appreciation that occurs when people re-evaluate an artwork across repeated trials.

Following the reviewer's guidance, we formulated a formal \textbf{Difference-in-Differences (DiD) linear mixed model}:
\begin{equation}
\text{Taste}_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Trial}_{it} + \sum_{k=2}^{K} \beta_k \text{Cond}_{ik} + \sum_{k=2}^{K} \delta_k (\text{Trial}_{it} \times \text{Cond}_{ik}) + u_i + \epsilon_{it}
\label{eq:did_model_resp}
\end{equation}
where the unexposed \textbf{Control Condition} (which received no information between trials) serves as the omitted reference category. Here, the interaction coefficients $\delta_k$ directly isolate the causal treatment effect relative to the counterfactual temporal drift:
\begin{equation*}
\delta_k = (\bar{Y}_{k, \text{Trial 2}} - \bar{Y}_{k, \text{Trial 1}}) - (\bar{Y}_{\text{Control}, \text{Trial 2}} - \bar{Y}_{\text{Control}, \text{Trial 1}})
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection*{2. Diagnosing What Changed: The ``False Positive'' Trap of Exposure Drift}
When we examined the unexposed \textbf{Control Condition} in our primary analytic sample (adults aged $\ge 24$, $N = 1,981$), the reviewer's intuition was confirmed: respondents who were given \textbf{zero information} nevertheless drifted upward substantially upon second viewing.
\begin{itemize}
    \item In the status-free baseline sample ($N = 457$), Control Condition respondents drifted upward by \textbf{$+0.156^{**}$} ($SE = 0.055, t = 2.86, p = 0.0044$).
    \item Across educational strata, exposure drift is heavily stratified: Postgraduates drift upward by \textbf{$+0.375^{**}$} ($SE = 0.137, p = 0.0064$) and Bachelor's degree holders by \textbf{$+0.149$} ($SE = 0.101, p = 0.138$), whereas non-degreed college attenders/AA holders drift only $+0.021$ ($p = 0.841$) and high school respondents drift $+0.053$ ($p = 0.651$).
    \item Across status consistency quadrants, Middle Class / College control respondents drift up by \textbf{$+0.250^{*}$} ($p = 0.025$) and Working Class / College by \textbf{$+0.200$} ($p = 0.259$), whereas non-college working-class respondents show virtually zero drift ($\beta_1 = +0.016, p = 0.871$).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Why did several positive results from the original draft lose statistical significance?} \\
In the original draft, we reported that respondents exposed to positive high-status cues showed raw upward shifts, which we interpreted as evidence of upward conformity. However, once we benchmark these raw shifts against their counterfactual control group---who drifted upward on their own without seeing any information---the net DiD estimates are statistically indistinguishable from zero (e.g., Bachelor's degree holders exposed to High-Status Likes: $\text{DiD} = -0.030, p = 0.809$). What appeared to be ``conformity'' in our original submission was almost entirely an artifact of natural exposure drift.

Furthermore, because DiD benchmarks against the Control Condition ($N = 157$ in the primary sample), standard errors appropriately incorporate the variance of the control group ($\text{Var}(\text{DiD}) = \text{Var}(\Delta_k) + \text{Var}(\Delta_{\text{Control}})$), increasing from $\approx 0.06$ to $\approx 0.11\text{--}0.15$. Consequently, weak positive drifts drop below conventional significance thresholds.

\subsubsection*{3. What Was Gained: New, Statistically Significant Causal Discoveries}
While spurious upward drift vanished, netting out the counterfactual exposure drift \textbf{uncovered major causal mechanisms that were completely invisible in the original submission}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Status-Based Distancing \& Bourdieusian Taste Abandonment (Hypothesis 5):} \\
    In the original draft, we lacked the resolution to observe status distancing. In our revised 4-tier educational analysis, respondents with a \textbf{Postgraduate Degree} exposed to \textit{Low-Status Likes} exhibit an immediate, severe evaluative collapse of nearly half a point: \textbf{$\text{DiD} = -0.491^{***}$ ($SE = 0.135, t = -3.64, p = 0.0003$, Cohen's $d = 0.33$)}---the single largest negative treatment effect in the entire experiment! Because postgraduates naturally drift upward by $+0.375$ under mere exposure, discovering that lower-status peers liked the artwork reverses their appreciation, providing vivid causal evidence of taste abandonment. Similarly, in the status consistency matrix, Middle Class / College respondents exposed to Low-Status Likes drop by \textbf{$\text{DiD} = -0.296^{*}$ ($SE = 0.144, p = 0.0391$)}.
    \item \textbf{Valence Asymmetry as a Suppressive Veto (Hypothesis 2b):} \\
    Across strata, negative evaluations act as an authoritative veto, completely overriding positive exposure drift:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Generalized Dislike (Status-Free Baseline):} $\text{DiD} = -0.186^{*}$ ($SE = 0.078, p = 0.0169$).
        \item \textit{High-Status Dislike (Bachelor's Tier):} $\text{DiD} = -0.271^{*}$ ($SE = 0.125, p = 0.0296$).
        \item \textit{Generalized Dislike (Bachelor's Tier):} $\text{DiD} = -0.308^{*}$ ($SE = 0.154, p = 0.0455$).
        \item \textit{High-Status Dislike (Postgraduate Tier):} $\text{DiD} = -0.266^{*}$ ($SE = 0.122, p = 0.0298$).
        \item \textit{Low-Status Dislike (Postgraduate Tier):} $\text{DiD} = -0.345^{**}$ ($SE = 0.123, p = 0.0051$).
        \item \textit{Generalized Dislike (Postgraduate Tier):} $\text{DiD} = -0.442^{**}$ ($SE = 0.151, p = 0.0035$).
    \end{itemize}
    In contrast, positive endorsements produce small, non-significant increments above control drift ($p \ge 0.55$). Negative feedback exerts a dependable veto on appreciation, while positive feedback is largely redundant.
    \item \textbf{Decisive Rejection of Cultural Goodwill (Hypothesis 4) \& Same-Status Alignment (Hypothesis 3):} \\
    Lower-educated respondents (High School or Less) are completely unresponsive to elite cues (High-Status Like: $\text{DiD} = +0.032, p = 0.777$; High-Status Dislike: $\text{DiD} = -0.140, p = 0.215$), displaying aesthetic autonomy rather than Bourdieusian deference. Similarly, respondents across all tiers fail to positively align with same-status peers (Bachelor's with High-Status Like: $\text{DiD} = -0.030, p = 0.809$; High School with Low-Status Like: $\text{DiD} = +0.051, p = 0.697$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Point 7: Observational Nature of Status Categories \& IPW Logic}

\begin{reviewercomment}
7. Although the information treatment was randomly assigned, membership in categories such as ``low--high'' and ``low--low'' is also determined by respondents' pre-existing education or subjective social class. For example, individuals with lower levels of education may be more likely to occupy a lower position than the reference group---another reason why the exact treatment wording should be provided. Consequently, respondents do not necessarily have the same probability of entering each combined-status category, and comparisons across these categories may partly capture pre-existing differences between respondents rather than only the causal effect of the experimental information. This issue is common in studies that provide respondents with information about their position in the social structure (e.g., Cruces et al., 2013). The authors acknowledge this problem and address it using inverse probability weighting (IPW), but it would be valuable to devote a few additional sentences to explaining the logic of this approach and its implications for causal interpretation. Such a discussion would be a valuable contribution to the field and would help readers understand the rationale for the analytical strategy.
\end{reviewercomment}

\textbf{Status:} \textcolor{forestgreen}{\textbf{[Addressed]}} \\
\textbf{Location in Revised Manuscript:} Section 2.6 (Inverse Probability Weighting), Figure 2

\textbf{Response / Actions Taken:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item We are deeply grateful to the reviewer for raising this vital methodological distinction and for pointing us to the insightful framing in Cruces et al.~(2013).
    \item In Section 2.6 of the revised manuscript (\texttt{manuscript\_R1.tex}), we have added a dedicated, pedagogical explanation of the hybrid observational-experimental setup, the formal mechanics of Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW), and its exact implications for causal interpretation:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{The Structural Confounding Problem:} While the experimental treatments (peer like vs.\ dislike and peer status vignettes) were randomized, respondents' own structural status locations (objective education $\times$ subjective class) are observational attributes. Consequently, respondents from different status groups differ systematically on baseline demographic characteristics---for instance, Middle Class college graduates are substantially older and disproportionately have college-educated parents compared to non-college Working Class respondents. Unadjusted comparisons across status groups risk attributing differences in evaluative shifts to social class when they may instead reflect demographic confounding.
        \item \textbf{Pedagogical Mechanics of IPW:} Following Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983), we address this in two formal steps:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textit{Step 1 (Generalized Propensity Scores):} We fit a multinomial logistic regression predicting each respondent's probability of belonging to their observed status consistency quadrant $S_i \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ (or educational group) conditional on exogenous background covariates $\mathbf{X}_i$ (age, gender, race/ethnicity, and parental educational capital):
            \begin{equation*}
            e_s(\mathbf{X}_i) = P(S_i = s \mid \mathbf{X}_i)
            \end{equation*}
            \item \textit{Step 2 (Constructing Balancing Weights):} We assign each respondent a weight equal to the inverse of their estimated propensity score:
            \begin{equation*}
            w_i = \frac{1}{e_s(\mathbf{X}_i)} = \frac{1}{P(S_i = s \mid \mathbf{X}_i)}
            \end{equation*}
        \end{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Intuitive Logic \& Pseudo-Population:} Intuitively, IPW creates a synthetic \textbf{``pseudo-population''} by up-weighting underrepresented demographic profiles within each status group (e.g., working-class individuals whose parents attended college) and down-weighting overrepresented profiles. This severs the statistical dependency between the baseline covariates $\mathbf{X}_i$ and status group membership.
        \item \textbf{Causal Interpretation:} Under the standard assumptions of conditional exchangeability (no unmeasured confounders given $\mathbf{X}_i$) and positivity ($0 < e_s(\mathbf{X}_i) < 1$), the weighted pseudo-population satisfies covariate balance. Consequently, subsequent differences in evaluative drift, behavioral inertia (``staying''), and reactive distancing across status categories can be causally attributed to structural status location and consistency, rather than demographic sorting.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}
