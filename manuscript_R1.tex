\title{Stay, React, or Conform? Examining Social Influence Effects on Aesthetic Judgments}
\author{Omar Lizardo \& Sara Skiles}
\date{Revised Manuscript --- \today}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
In this study, we address the question, foundational to sociological studies of taste, of the extent to which others' aesthetic judgments causally influence individual evaluations of cultural works, and how this influence depends on the social distance between self and other. While past research has documented observational associations between cultural tastes and social networks, the underlying causal mechanisms—whether individuals actively conform, react, or remain anchored in their initial judgments—have rarely been submitted to experimental test. Using a national survey experiment ($N = 2,275$), we manipulate exposure to others' aesthetic evaluations (likes vs.\ dislikes) and the status characteristics of the evaluating group across repeated trials. Using a difference-in-differences framework and inverse probability weighting, we find substantial evidence of \textit{valence asymmetry}: while negative evaluations from others broadly suppress positive exposure drift across groups, positive evaluations facilitate upward shifts, conditional on status congruence. Furthermore, analyzing discrete behavioral choices shows that evaluative stability (``staying'') is not driven by high status alone, but by \textit{structural status consistency}: both high-consistent and low-consistent individuals display high evaluative inertia, whereas status-inconsistent individuals exhibit evaluative fluidity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Personal Tastes versus Others' Tastes}

Taste expressions and consumption have long been shown to be a function of individuals' socially induced motivation to differentiate themselves from others \citep{simmel1957, dimaggio1987}. \citet{bourdieu1984} suggests that taste differences evoke a visceral reaction and that individuals engage in symbolic boundary work and status distinction by rejecting others' tastes. In this way, expressing \textit{dis}tastes serves to communicate social distance from others \citep{bryson1996, lamont2002}. Accordingly, in contexts where individuals discover an unexpected similarity between their taste and that of members of an outgroup, they abandon these tastes to maintain social boundaries \citep{berger2008, simmel1957}.

However, other people's tastes do not have to be solely a source of distinction and separation; they can also be a basis for building solidarity and discovering shared perspectives, especially among people considered similar in some socially relevant way. Via this process, expressing shared likes and dislikes can signal affiliation with others and serve as a mechanism for creating bounded groups \citep{dimaggio1987, lizardo2006}. Accordingly, contexts in which individuals find a \textit{similarity} of tastes with members of their ingroup can foster community, solidarity, and productive exchange \citep{wohl2015community-2d2, vanzella2022tastes}. 

Note that in both stories, it is presumed that cultural choices and judgments of taste are either directly or indirectly shaped by others' tastes. In the first story, the tastes of others are a source of distinction and separation, while in the second story, they are a source of affiliation and solidarity. In both cases, the tastes of others are presumed to have a causal effect on our own tastes. Yet, despite the centrality of this assumption to sociological theories of taste, there is surprisingly little empirical evidence that others' tastes causally influence our own. Perhaps because experimental methods capable of isolating this causal influence by design are still relatively uncommon in the field, despite a flurry of recent work addressing the cause. In this paper, we address this gap by experimentally testing the causal effect of others' tastes on our own aesthetic evaluations.

Accordingly, in the research reported in this paper, we ask: \textit{How does access to the evaluations influence our own aesthetic evaluations of cultural objects? }


\subsection{How Do Others' Tastes Influence Our Own?}

\subsubsection{The Generalized Influence Mechanism}

The simplest answer to the question we posed sees the production of aesthetic judgments as a special case of judgment under uncertainty. This means that information about other people's judgments becomes a key cog in a \textit{generalized influence mechanism} \citep{salganik2006experimental-88d, childress2012cultural-0cc}. 

The basic idea behind generalized influence is simple: in any context where there is uncertainty about how to evaluate a cultural object, access to information about how others have evaluated it provides a heuristic for resolving that uncertainty, such that going along with the crowd is not necessarily a sub-optimal strategy \citep{strang2001search-10a, mark2003culture-ed5}. According to this view, individuals tend to adjust their own evaluations in a direction consistent with the valence of others' evaluations. For instance, discovering that a cultural object is broadly endorsed by others (regardless of those others' characteristics) often increases its appeal (Positive Generalized Alignment), while discovering it is disliked decreases its appeal (Negative Generalized Alignment), as has been shown in previous work \citep{salganik2006experimental-88d}.

However, cultural influence is rarely so monolithic; the status of the ``others" matters. Exposure to the preferences of a high-status group might induce \textit{conformity} (alignment), whereas exposure to a low-status or out-group might induce \textit{reactance} (distancing). This approach is consistent with traditional models in the sociology of culture and consumption, which use a balance-like logic \citep{heider1946attitudes-ff3}---e.g., if a group you want to distance yourself from likes something then you dislike it---to predict how information about other people's choices (either explicit or implied) affects judgments of taste. These models suggest that access to positive evaluations from others will increase subjective evaluations of worth when those others are positively regarded, but not when they are negatively regarded. In the same way, access to other people’s negative evaluations will lead to a decrease in subjective evaluations of worth when those others are positively regarded \citep{lizardo2016cultural-aaa}. Here influence operates as either conformity or ``goodwill" \citep{bourdieu1984} when the presumed others are high-status, or as rejection or ``symbolic boundary drawing" when others are low-status \citep{bryson1996}. 

The ``Bourdieusian'' \citep {bourdieu1984} version of this model adds the complicating factor of the focal person's own position in the social space, suggesting that goodwill and conformity to the opinions of presumed high-status others should be more prevalent among lower status respondents, while resistance and distancing from the judgments of presumed low-status others should be more prevalent among higher status persons \citep{lizardo2016cultural-aaa}. While the Bourdieusian symbolic power model predicts goodwill (conformity on the part of lower-status persons to the judgments of high-status others) and equally possible pattern of results is that of generalized reactance, or the symbolic ``war of all against all'' where low-status others distance their judgments against those perceived to be of higher status, while higher-status people do the same against those of perceived lower status \cite{tampubolon2008revisiting}.

\subsection{Study Description}

In what follows, we put these considerations to the test by specifically examining how the respondent's objective and subjective class status interact with a fictional set of others' class status to produce three theoretically distinct outcomes: \textit{staying} (resisting influence), conforming (influence as a form of alignment), or reacting (influence as a form of distancing).

To do so, we designed a survey-experimental study to disentangle three distinct social influence mechanisms shaping aesthetic taste for a cultural object. In our study, participants were asked to tell us how they felt about a painting---Whistler's \textit{Nocturne}; m see Figure~\ref{fig:stimulus}---on a scale ranging from ``dislike it very much'' to ``like it very much.'' After we obtained this evaluation, in the experimental part of the survey, we told a subset of participants that our survey had so far shown that the group of participants that was most likely to either like (for one subset of participants) or dislike (for another subset) was either people with a PhD and working in higher education, people holding a bachelor's degree in business and working in financial investment, or people with a high school education and working in food service. Yet a third group of participants was simply told that an unmarked set of other people who had taken the survey had liked or disliked the painting (a final group of participants was not provided with any feedback). All participants then spent between 10 and 20 minutes answering various sociodemographic questions and completing survey modules. Participants were then shown the same painting one last time, prompted to take another look, and asked to provide their final take on it, using the same evaluative categories as in the beginning. 

\subsection{Hypotheses}
\subsubsection{Mere Exposure and Positive Drift}
For participants in the Control Condition who are simply asked to rate the painting twice without being exposed to the opinions of others, repeated exposure to a cultural object across trials is expected to produce an upward drift in evaluation even in the absence of external social information, a phenomenon in psychology known as the \textit{mere exposure effect} \citep{zajonc1968attitudinal, reber1998effects-efe}.\footnote{For an application of the mere exposure effect to historical dynamics in the aesthetic evaluation of art see \citet{cutting2003gustave}.} 

Accordingly, our baseline hypothesis is that we should observe \textit{positive drift} in the Control Condition, with participants asked to judge the same cultural object twice being more likely to produce a more positive judgment (on average) the second time. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 1 (Mere Exposure Effect):} \textit{Participants who are asked to judge the painting twice, without being exposed to the opinions, will be more likely to give a more positive judgment the second time.}
\end{itemize}

Thus, if the mere exposure mechanism is at work, the Control Condition should show a positive drift in evaluation across trials, which we can use as a baseline to compare against the treatment conditions in which participants are exposed to the opinions. 

\subsubsection{Social Influence and Generalized Alignment}

Generalized positive or negative influence processes should operate in tandem with mere exposure, either enhancing it (in the positive generalized influence condition) or suppressing it (in the negative generalized influence condition). In other words, we should expect that exposure to positive evaluations from others will enhance the positive drift in evaluation across trials, whereas exposure to negative evaluations from others will suppress it \citep{salganik2006experimental-88d}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 2a (Positive Evaluations):} \textit{Endorsements (liking) from generalized others should exert an overall positive influence on the respondent's second evaluation, facilitating baseline drift.}
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 2b (Negative Evaluations):} \textit{Disapproval (disliking) from generalized others should exert an overall negative influence on the respondent's second evaluation, blocking the mere exposure effect.}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Social Influence and Status Specific Alignment}

When it comes to hypotheses having to do with the interaction between a person's own status and the perceived status of others who either like or dislike the painting, we can consider three possibilities:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Hypothesis 3 (Same-Status Alignment):} \textit{Individuals will tend to align their judgments with those of others who are perceived to be of similar status, such that they will tend to move towards more positive evaluations when provided information that similar-status others liked the cultural object, and towards negative evaluations when provided information that similar-status others disliked the painting.}
\item \textbf{Hypothesis 4 (Cultural Goodwill):} \textit{Lower-status individuals will tend to align their judgments with those of higher-status others, moving towards more positive evaluations when provided information that higher-status others liked the cultural object, and towards more negative evaluations when provided information that higher-status others disliked the cultural object.}
\item \textbf{Hypothesis 5 (Status-Based Distancing Disalignment):} \textit{Individuals will tend to distance themselves from the judgments of others who are perceived to be of different status, such that they will tend to move towards more negative evaluations when provided information that higher-status others liked the cultural object, and towards more positive evaluations when provided information that higher-status others disliked the painting.}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Status Inconsistency and Aesthetic Malleability}

The previous hypotheses presumed that individuals occupy a single, consistent status position, either high or low. However, in reality, many individuals occupy a status position that is inconsistent across different dimensions of social stratification. For instance, a person may be working class but have a college degree, or middle class but without one. In the classic literature on the subject, this is usually referred to as status consistency and status crystallization \citep{hope1975models}. 

The basic expectation stemming from this literature, along with more contemporary work on the social psychology of status \citep{correll2003expectations}, is that individuals with consistent status profiles will be more likely to maintain their initial evaluation (``staying'') than those with inconsistent status profiles, who will be more likely to revise their initial evaluation in response to information about the aesthetic opinions of others. The reason for this is that status crystallization provides people with assurance as to their judgments (inuring them from influence) while individuals caught in contradictory slots in the social structure will be more likely to be uncertain about their own judgments and thus more susceptible to influence.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 6 (Status Consistency):} \textit{Individuals with consistent status profiles (either high or low) will be more likely to maintain their initial evaluation (``staying'') than those with inconsistent status profiles, who will be more likely to revise their initial evaluation in response to information about the aesthetic opinions of others.}
\end{itemize}


\section{Data and Methods}

\subsection{Sample Recruitment and Survey Administration}
Data for this study were collected between February 28 and March 6, 2012, via an online experimental survey administered by Survey Sampling International (SSI), a leading research firm specializing in online panel recruitment and survey sampling. A total of 3,782 panel members accessed the survey instrument. Of these, 97 were excluded due to lack of informed consent ($N = 85$) or age under 18 ($N = 12$), 1,394 were screened out because demographic quota targets had already been met, and 16 dropped out during early screens, resulting in an analytic sample of $N = 2,275$ completed responses (with $N = 2,253$ providing complete initial and post-treatment evaluations). Participants received monetary compensation (\$3.92) via SSI, funded by an NSF Doctoral Dissertation Improvement Grant (Award SES-1203426) and the University of Notre Dame Institute for Scholarship in Liberal Arts.

To give respondents an opportunity to complete higher education and eliminate right-censoring in educational attainment among individuals still of standard undergraduate age (18--24), our primary analytic sample restricts the population to adults aged 24 or older (specifically, $\ge 25$ given standard five-year Census survey brackets; $N = 1,981$, yielding $3,962$ repeated observations across trials). In this mature adult population, educational attainment reflects completed institutional cultural capital, allowing for an unambiguous test of status-based influence without conflating non-completion with non-enrollment. As documented in Appendix \ref{sec:appendix_full_sample}, re-estimating all models on the unrestricted full sample ($N = 2,253$) yields identical substantive findings and theoretical verdicts.

The sampling frame employed dynamic quota matching calibrated to U.S. Census benchmarks for gender, age, race/ethnicity, and geographic region to achieve a diverse and quasi-representative national sample of U.S. adults. To ensure adequate statistical power for examining status-differentiated cultural dynamics---particularly within high-cultural-capital occupational cells---the sample incorporated a deliberate oversample of college graduates (43.3\% holding a bachelor's degree or higher in the primary analytic sample versus 40.5\% in the full sample and approximately 28\% nationally in 2012). \subsection{Experimental Design and Stimulus}
All respondents viewed an image of the same artwork: \textit{Nocturne: Blue and Gold -- Old Battersea Bridge} by James McNeill Whistler (1872), displayed in Figure \ref{fig:stimulus}. The painting was selected based on an online pilot study ($N=100$) that tested eight artworks; the Whistler painting was chosen because it produced the highest initial pre-treatment variance in aesthetic evaluations across all demographic subgroups.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{figures/experimental_stimulus.jpeg}
    \caption{Experimental Stimulus: \textit{Nocturne: Blue and Gold -- Old Battersea Bridge}, by James McNeill Whistler (1872)}
    \label{fig:stimulus}
\end{figure}

The experiment used an incomplete factorial design with within-subject pre- and post-trials. In Trial 1 (pre-treatment baseline), respondents viewed the artwork accompanied by the prompt:
\begin{quote}
\textit{``Please look at this painting for a few seconds.''} \\
\textit{``Give us your first impression of this painting -- how much do you like it?''}
\end{quote}
Responses were recorded using a 7-point Likert scale (ranging from 1 = ``I like it very much'' to 7 = ``I dislike it very much'', along with an ``I can't see the image'' screening option; reverse-coded so that 7 = ``like very much'' and 1 = ``dislike very much''). Next, during an intervening survey block, respondents completed demographic questions and a musical taste battery before treatment respondents read a brief vignette describing how a group of other people evaluated the artwork. Finally, in Trial 2 (post-treatment final impression), respondents were shown the painting again and asked:
\begin{quote}
\textit{``Please look at this painting for a few seconds again.''} \\
\textit{``Give us your final impression of this painting -- how much do you like it?''}
\end{quote}
on the identical rating scale. (The complete verbatim wording, layout, and sequencing across all survey screens are transcribed in Appendix \ref{sec:appendix_vignettes}.)

To prevent terminological confusion, we distinguish between the \textit{pre-treatment baseline measurement} (Trial 1, which captures initial, uninfluenced aesthetic preference) and the \textit{Control Condition} (the experimental group that receives no information about others between trials, allowing us to observe pure temporal re-evaluation in the absence of treatment). We refer to changes over repeated trials in this unexposed group as \textit{exposure drift}.

\subsection{Mitigation of Experimenter Demand Characteristics and Respondent Reactivity}
A common methodological concern in within-subject experimental designs is whether respondents become aware that an evaluative shift is being measured, inducing experimenter demand compliance or artificial consistency. We implemented four procedural and design safeguards to mitigate demand awareness and assess respondent reactivity.

First, Trial 1 and Trial 2 were separated by an extensive cognitive and temporal buffer comprising 15 to 20 minutes of substantive survey batteries. Between aesthetic evaluations, respondents completed detailed modules on educational history, parental degree attainment, employment status, 22-category Standard Occupational Classification (SOC) coding, annual household income, and a comprehensive 20-genre musical taste battery evaluating preference ratings, listening frequency, and listener demographic stereotypes. This substantial intervening distance prevented respondents from mechanically recalling or feeling compelled to repeat their Trial 1 ratings.

Second, the experimental feedback screens were framed naturalistically as observational descriptive summaries from prior survey waves rather than prescriptive social norms: respondents were told, \textit{``Our survey so far indicates that the group of people most likely to say that they [LIKE / DISLIKE] the painting you saw have completed\dots''} Immediately following this prompt, respondents completed attribution and subjective similarity probes concerning the evaluating group, maintaining the perception that the survey was investigating social perception rather than testing their personal susceptibility to persuasion. When respondents subsequently reached the end of the survey and were re-shown the painting, they were simply asked for their ``final impression'' using the identical rating scale, without being reminded of their initial Trial 1 score and without any instructional prompt linking their second rating to the opinions of others or suggesting that conformity was expected.

Third, in the following analyses, all treatment shifts are compared against the unexposed Control Condition. Because control respondents completed the identical intervening survey battery without receiving any cue from others, comparing treatment conditions against this baseline cleanly separates genuine informational influence from any residual re-test reactivity or temporal fatigue.

Finally, the empirical distribution of discrete behavioral choices provides strong evidence against demand compliance. Over 74\% of respondents across conditions exhibited complete evaluative stability (``staying''), and a substantial proportion displayed active oppositional reactance by shifting in the direction opposite to the cue. Had respondents been compliance-driven, conformity rates would have dominated across conditions, rather than emerging selectively in status-inconsistent configurations.

\subsection{Variables and Measurement}
Aesthetic evaluation was measured on a reversed 1--7 scale. In continuous models, the outcome is the repeated evaluation across trials. In behavioral models, change between trials is categorized into three mutually exclusive choices: \textit{Stay} ($\Delta = 0$), \textit{Conform} (moving in the direction of others' evaluations), and \textit{React} (moving in the opposite direction).

The treatment conditions crossed others' evaluation (Like vs.\ Dislike) with others' social status. High-status others were differentiated into Cultural Specialists (possessing multiple advanced degrees including a PhD and working in higher education) and Economic Specialists (holding a bachelor's degree in business and working in financial investment). Low-status others were described as possessing a high school education and working in food service. In addition, two Taste-Only conditions provided other like-or-dislike evaluations without any status indicators, and a pure Control Condition provided no information about others between trials ($N=157$ in the primary sample; $N=182$ in the full sample).

We collapse the Economic Specialist and Cultural Specialist conditions into a single High-Status condition (\textit{+Status}) for the primary analysis. This consolidation is justified on both statistical and substantive grounds. First, crossing respondents' own status consistency quadrants by all nine experimental conditions would expand the design to 36 cells, reducing cell sizes in smaller subgroups to $N \approx 20$--$35$ (and $N = 21$ in control), severely inflating the Minimum Detectable Effect threshold ($d > 0.45$). Second, formal linear hypothesis tests confirm that responses to Economic Specialists and Cultural Specialists are statistically indistinguishable for both positive cues ($F = 0.29, p = 0.591$ in the pooled sample; $F = 0.92, p = 0.337$ among college graduates) and negative cues ($F = 0.06, p = 0.803$ in the pooled sample; $F = 0.04, p = 0.846$ among college graduates). Collapsing them into a single high-status category effectively doubles the statistical power for high-status cues ($N \approx 505$ per arm in the primary sample and $N \approx 575$ in the full sample) without obscuring any empirical heterogeneity.

To capture respondents' structural social location, we examine objective educational attainment (College Degree vs.\ No College) and subjective class self-identification (Middle Class vs.\ Working Class). Methodologically, we analyze these dimensions stepwise: we first evaluate the separate moderating effects of educational attainment alone and subjective class identification alone (Section \ref{sec:stepwise_models}, Figure \ref{fig:did_edu_class}, and Table \ref{tbl:did_edu_class}) before examining their intersection in a four-quadrant typology of status consistency: Working Class without College, Working Class with College, Middle Class without College, and Middle Class with College. In the base operationalization, ``Working Class'' includes a minority of respondents who self-identified as ``Lower Class'' ($N = 220$, 11.1\%) and ``Middle Class'' includes a small proportion of respondents who self-identified as ``Upper Class'' ($N = 47$, 2.4\%). To verify that our substantive conclusions are not sensitive to boundaries of subjective class, we fit models using an alternative categorization that restricts the sample only to self-identified working- and middle-class respondents (excluding lower- and upper-class extremes) as an additional sensitivity analysis in Appendix \ref{sec:appendix_class_robustness}.

\subsection{Inverse Probability Weighting}
A key methodological challenge in experimental design examining status-contingent influences stems from the hybrid nature of the design. While the experimental cues (others' evaluation valence and others' status) were randomly assigned, respondents' own social locations—captured by the crossing of educational attainment and subjective class identification—represent pre-existing, observational attributes \citep{cruces2013biases}. Consequently, individuals do not have an equal probability of belonging to each status group: for example, Middle Class individuals with a college degree are systematically older, more likely to have college-educated parents, and differ in racial/ethnic composition compared to non-college Working Class individuals. If unaddressed, comparisons across status groups (and their interactions with the randomized treatments) risk confounding the effect of social class with these correlated background characteristics.

To solve this problem and strengthen causal inference without inducing overcontrol bias (avoiding conditioning on post-treatment socioeconomic outcomes of education, such as personal income or adult migration), we implement Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW) using strictly exogenous pre-treatment social background characteristics \citep{rosenbaum1983central}. The logic of IPW proceeds in two steps. First, for each respondent, we estimate a propensity model predicting status group membership conditional on exogenous baseline covariates $\mathbf{X}_i$ (age, gender, race/ethnicity, and parental educational capital): $e_s(\mathbf{X}_i) = P(S_i = s \mid \mathbf{X}_i)$. For the four-quadrant status consistency models, we estimate generalized multinomial propensity scores; for the univariate moderation models (education alone and subjective class alone), we estimate targeted binary logistic propensity scores specifically balancing each respective dimension. Second, each observation is weighted by the inverse of its estimated propensity score to target the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) across the sample: $w_i = 1 / e_s(\mathbf{X}_i) = 1 / P(S_i = s \mid \mathbf{X}_i)$.

Intuitively, IPW creates a synthetic ``pseudo-population'' by up-weighting underrepresented demographic profiles within each status group (e.g., working-class respondents whose parents completed college) and down-weighting overrepresented profiles. This weighting scheme breaks the empirical association between the baseline covariates $\mathbf{X}_i$ and status group membership. Under the assumption of conditional exchangeability (no unmeasured confounders given $\mathbf{X}_i$) and positivity, IPW ensures that status groups are demographically comparable. As a result, subsequent differences in evaluative shifts, behavioral inertia, or reactance across status categories can be interpreted as the direct consequence of structural status location and status consistency, rather than demographic artifacts.

Figure \ref{fig:balance} displays the standardized mean differences (SMD) for all baseline covariates across the status categories before and after Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW), generated using \texttt{cobalt} \citep{greifer2022cobalt}. Prior to weighting, status groups exhibited substantial demographic disparities: for instance, college-educated middle-class respondents were older and had higher rates of parental college attainment (raw SMDs ranging from 0.35 to 0.50). Following IPW estimation, the weighted pseudo-population achieved balance, with all absolute standardized mean differences compressed well below the conventional conservative threshold of 0.10 standard deviations. This confirms that subsequent model estimates isolate the effect of social class and status consistency, net of observed demographic sorting.

\subsection{Difference-in-Differences Analytic Strategy}
To strictly isolate information-induced shifts from exposure drift, we formulate our analysis within a Difference-in-Differences (DiD) framework. Rather than examining simple pre-to-post changes within each condition in isolation—which conflates the effect of information from others with the natural exposure drift that occurs upon viewing an artwork a second time—the DiD strategy compares every treatment condition against the influence-unexposed Control Condition.

Accordingly, we specify a linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts of the general form:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\text{Taste}_{it} = {} & \beta_0 + \beta_{1} \text{Trial}_{it} + \sum_{k=2}^{K} \beta_{k} \text{Cond}_{ik} + \sum_{k=2}^{K} \delta_{k} (\text{Trial}_{it} \times \text{Cond}_{ik}) + u_i + \epsilon_{it}
\end{split}
\label{eq:did_model}
\end{equation}
where $i$ indexes individual respondents ($i = 1, \dots, N$), $t \in \{1, 2\}$ indexes measurement trials (Trial 1 pre-treatment baseline vs.\ Trial 2 post-treatment evaluation), $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ indexes the experimental conditions with the unexposed Control Condition ($k = 1$) serving as the omitted reference category, $u_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_u)$ is the individual-specific random intercept, and $\epsilon_{it} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_\epsilon)$ is the trial-level residual error.

In the Equation~\ref{eq:did_model} specification, the intercept ($\beta_0$) represents the expected baseline aesthetic evaluation at Trial 1 (pre-treatment) for respondents assigned to the unexposed Control Condition: $\beta_0 = E[\text{Taste} \mid \text{Trial} = 1, \text{Control}]$. The Trial main effect ($\beta_{1}$) captures the counterfactual \textit{exposure drift} from Trial 1 to Trial 2 in the absence of external information from others: $\beta_{1} = E[\text{Taste} \mid \text{Trial} = 2, \text{Control}] - E[\text{Taste} \mid \text{Trial} = 1, \text{Control}]$. When repeated viewing induces an intrinsic appreciation of the artwork (the mere exposure effect), $\beta_{1}$ should be positive and statistically significant.

The condition main effects ($\beta_k$) reflect pre-treatment differences between condition $k$ and the Control Condition at Trial 1: $\beta_k = E[\text{Taste} \mid \text{Trial} = 1, \text{Cond} = k] - E[\text{Taste} \mid \text{Trial} = 1, \text{Control}]$. Because respondents were randomly assigned to experimental conditions before viewing the painting, these coefficients assess baseline balance and are expected to be close to zero. The interaction coefficients ($\delta_k$) represent the \textit{causal Difference-in-Differences treatment effects}. Each $\delta_k$ measures whether the change in evaluations from Trial 1 to Trial 2 in treatment condition $k$ differed significantly from the change observed in the unexposed Control Condition:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\delta_k = {} & \left( E[\text{Taste} \mid \text{Trial} = 2, k] - E[\text{Taste} \mid \text{Trial} = 1, k] \right) \\
              & - \left( E[\text{Taste} \mid \text{Trial} = 2, \text{Control}] - E[\text{Taste} \mid \text{Trial} = 1, \text{Control}] \right)
\end{split}
\end{equation}

Substantively, $\delta_k$ answers the counterfactual question: \textit{Did exposure to evaluation $k$ cause respondents' aesthetic judgments to change significantly more or less than they would have changed under mere repeated exposure alone?} A positive estimate ($\delta_k > 0$) indicates that feedback from others facilitated aesthetic appreciation beyond natural exposure drift, whereas a negative estimate ($\delta_k < 0$) indicates that feedback from others suppressed or reversed the positive exposure drift that would have naturally occurred.

Estimating the simple pooled DiD model in Equation~\ref{eq:did_model} without differentiating by social location yields mostly muted or null effects (reported in Appendix Table A.2 and Appendix Figure A.2). As our introductory considerations suggest, however, this pooled invariance is an artifact of aggregation, hiding underlying heterogeneity in the causal effect \citep{brand2013causal}. In essence, because aesthetic influence operates relationally based on the status congruence between ego and alter, pooling across socially dissimilar respondents washes out opposing directional adjustments. The substantive dynamics of social influence emerge when we examine models interacting experimental conditions with education, subjective social class, and status consistency.

Instead, to test hypotheses of how social influence is moderated by respondents' structural social location (educational attainment, subjective social class, and status consistency), we extend Equation \ref{eq:did_model} to a fully moderated Difference-in-Differences specification:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\text{Taste}_{it} = {} & \beta_0 + \beta_{1} \text{Trial}_{it} + \sum_{k=2}^{K} \beta_{k} \text{Cond}_{ik} + \sum_{m=2}^{M} \gamma_m \text{Status}_{im} \\
& + \sum_{k=2}^{K} \delta_k (\text{Trial}_{it} \times \text{Cond}_{ik}) + \sum_{m=2}^{M} \theta_m (\text{Trial}_{it} \times \text{Status}_{im}) \\
& + \sum_{k=2}^{K} \sum_{m=2}^{M} \lambda_{km} (\text{Cond}_{ik} \times \text{Status}_{im}) \\
& + \sum_{k=2}^{K} \sum_{m=2}^{M} \psi_{km} (\text{Trial}_{it} \times \text{Cond}_{ik} \times \text{Status}_{im}) + u_i + \epsilon_{it}
\end{split}
\label{eq:did_moderation_model}
\end{equation}

Where $\text{Status}_{im}$ represents indicators for respondent $i$'s structural social location across $M$ categories ($m = 1, \dots, M$, with $m = 1$ denoting the reference group). In this moderated framework, $\theta_m$ captures group-specific differences in counterfactual baseline exposure drift in the Control Condition ($\text{Trial}_{it} \times \text{Status}_{im}$), $\delta_k$ represents the DiD treatment effect for the reference status group, and $\psi_{km}$ represents the three-way interaction coefficient ($\text{Trial}_{it} \times \text{Cond}_{ik} \times \text{Status}_{im}$) testing whether the causal DiD treatment effect of condition $k$ diverges significantly across status groups. The status-specific causal DiD treatment effect for subgroup $m$ is given by the linear combination $\delta_{km} = \delta_k + \psi_{km}$.\footnote{By the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem, estimating Equation \ref{eq:did_model} (or Equation \ref{eq:did_moderation_model}) via linear mixed models with individual random intercepts ($u_i$) is mathematically equivalent to regressing individual change scores ($\Delta \text{Taste}_i = \text{Taste}_{i2} - \text{Taste}_{i1}$) on the treatment and status interaction terms with the Control Condition as the omitted reference group:
\begin{equation}
\Delta \text{Taste}_i = \beta_{1} + \sum_{m=2}^{M} \theta_m \text{Status}_{im} + \sum_{k=2}^{K} \delta_k \text{Cond}_{ik} + \sum_{k=2}^{K} \sum_{m=2}^{M} \psi_{km} (\text{Cond}_{ik} \times \text{Status}_{im}) + \epsilon_i
\end{equation}
where the intercept and status main effects estimate the control exposure drift within each status group, and the interaction terms estimate the status-specific DiD treatment effects.} 

\section{Results}

\subsection{Baseline Exposure Drift and Generalized Social Influence}
We begin by establishing a status-free empirical baseline: testing simultaneously for any mere-exposure drift observed under repeated evaluation in the unexposed Control Condition and examining whether learning that unknown others liked or disliked the artwork shifts aggregate aesthetic evaluations via a generalized influence mechanism. To do so, we estimate a repeated-measures linear mixed model on the subset of respondents assigned to the Control Condition ($N = 157$), the Generalized Like condition ($N = 152$), and the Generalized Dislike condition ($N = 148$), totaling $N = 457$ respondents ($914$ observations across trials) with Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW):

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\text{Taste}_{it} = {} & \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Trial}_{it} + \beta_2 \text{Like}_i + \beta_3 \text{Dislike}_i \\
& + \delta_{\text{Like}} (\text{Trial}_{it} \times \text{Like}_i) + \delta_{\text{Dislike}} (\text{Trial}_{it} \times \text{Dislike}_i) + u_i + \epsilon_{it}
\end{split}
\label{eq:generalized_did_model}
\end{equation}

Where the unexposed Control Condition serves as the omitted reference group. Figure \ref{fig:did_baseline} visualizes these baseline dynamics: Panel A displays the evaluative trajectories across repeated trials, illustrating the positive mere exposure drift in the Control Condition alongside the treatment conditions, while Panel B reports the net Difference-in-Differences treatment effects relative to this counterfactual drift.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/Figure_DiD_Baseline_Influence.png}
    \caption{Baseline Exposure Drift and Generalized Social Influence (Status-Free Primary Sample, $N = 457$). Panel A displays mean aesthetic evaluations across repeated trials with 95\% confidence intervals (IPW-weighted) for the Control Condition, Generalized Like, and Generalized Dislike. Panel B displays net Difference-in-Differences treatment effects ($\delta_k$) relative to the Control Condition exposure drift (dashed line at zero), with 95\% confidence intervals. Colors indicate statistical significance: red ($p < 0.05$) and grey ($p \ge 0.10$).}
    \label{fig:did_baseline}
\end{figure}

This status-free baseline model establishes two main findings. First, consistent with \textit{Hypothesis 1 (Mere Exposure Effect)}, in the complete absence of external social cues (the Control Condition), repeated exposure generates a statistically significant upward appreciation of the artwork ($\beta_1 = +0.156, SE = 0.055, t = 2.86, p = 0.0044$), confirming that mere exposure alone produces positive drift across trials. Second, evaluating generalized social influence reveals a pronounced \textit{valence asymmetry} between positive and negative consensus. When respondents are informed that unknown others liked the painting, raw evaluations increase from 4.65 to 4.85 ($+0.203, p = 0.009$); however, relative to the natural counterfactual drift observed in the control group, this additional increment is positive but not statistically distinguishable from mere exposure drift ($\delta_{\text{Like}} = +0.047, SE = 0.080, t = 0.59, p = 0.555$). Consequently, \textit{Hypothesis 2a (Positive Evaluations)} is not supported, as generalized positive cues fail to significantly facilitate evaluative drift beyond the baseline exposure effect.

In contrast, learning that others disliked the painting completely overrides the mere exposure effect, reducing second-trial evaluations to a flat $-0.030$ ($p = 0.68$). Relative to the control baseline drift, this constitutes a statistically significant negative Difference-in-Differences suppression ($\delta_{\text{Dislike}} = -0.186, SE = 0.078, t = -2.40, p = 0.0169$), providing strong empirical support for \textit{Hypothesis 2b (Negative Evaluations)}. Directly contrasting positive and negative cues reveals a significant directional swing of nearly a quarter point on the 7-point scale ($\Delta = +0.233, SE = 0.080, \chi^2 = 8.51, p = 0.0035$), demonstrating that while generalized endorsements fail to reliably boost appreciation over mere exposure, generalized disliking exerts a discernible suppressing effect.

While this status-free model demonstrates clear valence asymmetry overall, evaluating social influence solely at the aggregate level conceals substantial causal heterogeneity based on social location \citep{brand2013causal}.\footnote{As shown in the full pooled models in Appendix Table A.4 and Appendix Figure A.2.} We therefore turn to our moderated models to examine how social influence is conditioned by educational capital, subjective social class, and structural status consistency.

\subsection{Moderation by Educational Attainment and Subjective Class}
\label{sec:stepwise_models}
To examine how social influence works across distinct dimensions of class and status difference, we first inspect how educational attainment alone (College Degree vs.\ No College) and subjective social class alone (Working Class vs.\ Middle Class) moderate the experimental treatments, using models based on Equation~\ref{eq:did_moderation_model}. Figure \ref{fig:did_edu_class} visualizes these treatment effects with 95\% (red) and 90\% (orange) confidence intervals compared against the unexposed Control Condition.\footnote{Complete regression estimates are reported in Appendix Table \ref{tbl:did_edu_class}.}

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/Figure_DiD_Edu_Class_Separate.png}
    \caption{Difference-in-Differences Treatment Effects Moderated Separately by Educational Attainment (Panel A) and Subjective Social Class (Panel B). Horizontal bars depict point estimates with 95\% confidence intervals, compared with the unexposed Control Condition. Colors indicate statistical significance: red ($p < 0.05$), orange ($p < 0.10$), and grey ($p \ge 0.10$). Full regression estimates are provided in Appendix Table \ref{tbl:did_edu_class}.}
    \label{fig:did_edu_class}
\end{figure}

Looking at the separate models reveals that education is one of the primary structural fault lines governing responsiveness to external evaluations when evaluators are differentiated by status (Panel A of Figure \ref{fig:did_edu_class} and Appendix Table \ref{tbl:did_edu_class}). Non-college respondents tend to maintain or positively shift their evaluations: they exhibit positive shifts when informed that an unmarked majority liked the artwork ($\text{DiD} = +0.189, SE = 0.119, p = 0.112$) and when told that high-status others liked it ($\text{DiD} = +0.100, SE = 0.089, p = 0.264$), while remaining largely unresponsive to high-status dislikes ($\text{DiD} = -0.110, SE = 0.089, p = 0.216$).

In contrast, college-educated respondents are statistically significantly more likely to revise their evaluations downward, but never upward, across experimental conditions. In particular, college-educated respondents respond negatively in the High-Status Dislike condition ($\text{DiD} = -0.263$, $SE = 0.090$, $p = 0.0036$), the Low-Status Dislike condition ($\text{DiD} = -0.175$, $SE = 0.096$, $p = 0.0689$), and the Generalized Dislike condition ($\text{DiD} = -0.325$, $SE = 0.111$, $p = 0.0034$). As such, the highly educated seem to be sensitive mainly to negative evaluations of others. To inspect whether this pattern is robust across finer gradations of educational attainment, we also disaggregate education into four granular tiers (High School or Less, Two-Year / Some College, Bachelor's Degree, and Postgraduate Degree) in Appendix \ref{sec:appendix_edu_4tiers} (Figure \ref{fig:did_edu_4tiers} and Table \ref{tbl:did_edu_4tiers}), which demonstrates a clear monotonic gradient: sub-baccalaureate groups remain positive or inert, while negative suppression and status distancing emerge at the Bachelor's level and intensify dramatically among postgraduates. 

By comparison, examining subjective social class without regard to education (Panel B of Figure \ref{fig:did_edu_class}) produces a more diffuse picture. Self-identified working-class respondents tend to either revise their judgments downward or, more commonly, not at all, and, like college degree holders, are mainly sensitive to dislike information. Accordingly, working-class respondents show significant negative adjustments under Generalized Dislikes ($\text{DiD} = -0.248, SE = 0.107, p = 0.0200$) and a marginally significant negative shift under High-Status Dislikes ($\text{DiD} = -0.143, SE = 0.086, p = 0.0956$). 

Middle-class self-identifiers also exhibit predominantly negative or null shifts, but their behavior is consistent with both status-distancing and status-solidarity mechanisms. Accordingly, middle-class respondents display significant negative shifts under High-Status Dislike ($\text{DiD} = -0.204, SE = 0.091, p = 0.0249$) and Low-Status Like ($\text{DiD} = -0.179, SE = 0.101, p = 0.0752$)---thus demonstrating sensitivity to dislike information and taste abandonment under outgroup liking, alongside positive facilitation under Generalized Likes ($\text{DiD} = +0.111, SE = 0.113, p = 0.325$).

% Crucially, this comparison of education and subjective class identification, taken separately, is insufficient. Because objective credential attainment and subjective class identification cross-cut one another—nearly 28\% of working-class self-identifiers hold college degrees, while over 28\% of middle-class identifiers do not—univariate stratification conflates status-consistent actors with status-inconsistent actors. As we show below, the apparent lack of responsiveness among ``working-class'' respondents is an artifact of aggregating non-college workers (who exhibit normative inertia) with college-educated workers (who exhibit sharp reactivity against high-status cues). Examining their four-quadrant intersection is therefore essential for isolating the distinct mechanisms of status consistency and cross-status influence.

Evaluating our status-differentiated hypotheses through the DiD framework across these quadrants reveals which theoretical expectations are empirically supported versus unsupported.

First, Same-Status Alignment (\textit{Hypothesis 3}) is not supported. Hypothesis 3 predicted that individuals would align their judgments with the positive and negative evaluations of others sharing their status. When evaluated relative to the control condition counterfactual, neither high-status consistent respondents ($\text{DiD} = -0.142, SE = 0.127, p = 0.2625$) nor low-status consistent respondents ($\text{DiD} = -0.056, SE = 0.139, p = 0.6896$) show a statistically significant DiD shift when exposed to same-status likes. Similarly, low-status respondents exposed to same-status dislikes do not exhibit a statistically significant shift ($\text{DiD} = +0.149, SE = 0.140, p = 0.2884$). Because none of these estimates are statistically significant ($p \ge 0.10$), the data do not support Hypothesis 3.

Second, Cultural Goodwill (\textit{Hypothesis 4}) is not supported for low-status consistent individuals. Hypothesis 4 predicted that lower-status individuals would align their judgments with higher-status others, adopting their likes and conforming to their dislikes. Among low-status consistent respondents (Working Class/No College), neither exposure to High-Status Likes ($\text{DiD} = +0.097, SE = 0.120, p = 0.4204$) nor High-Status Dislikes ($\text{DiD} = -0.045, SE = 0.121, p = 0.7085$) produces a statistically significant change. Thus, Hypothesis 4 is not supported for low-status consistent individuals. However, for status-inconsistent workers who hold a college degree (Working Class/College), exposure to High-Status Dislikes produces a statistically significant negative DiD shift of $\text{DiD} = -0.320$ ($SE = 0.125, t = -2.56, p = 0.0106$), alongside a significant negative shift under Generalized Dislikes ($\text{DiD} = -0.349, SE = 0.156, p = 0.0251$). This indicates that sensitivity to high-status negative evaluation is concentrated among individuals who possess institutional cultural capital but identify with the working class.

Third, Status-Based Distancing Disalignment (\textit{Hypothesis 5}) is strongly supported. Netting out the counterfactual exposure drift provides direct empirical confirmation of Hypothesis 5. For high-status consistent respondents (Middle Class/College), exposure to Low-Status Likes (working-class others liking the painting) produces a statistically significant negative DiD divergence of $\text{DiD} = -0.308$ ($SE = 0.147, t = -2.09, p = 0.0362$) relative to the control condition. Because college-educated middle-class respondents naturally drift upward by $+0.246$ under mere exposure, discovering that working-class others liked the artwork actively reverses their appreciation. High-status consistent respondents also exhibit significant negative shifts under Generalized Dislikes ($\text{DiD} = -0.344, SE = 0.155, p = 0.0268$) and marginal negative shifts under High-Status Dislikes ($\text{DiD} = -0.243, SE = 0.131, p = 0.0643$) and Low-Status Dislikes ($\text{DiD} = -0.239, SE = 0.139, p = 0.0852$). This confirms that high-status individuals distance their judgments from low-status endorsements, providing experimental evidence of Bourdieusian symbolic distinction and taste abandonment.

Finally, regarding status-inconsistent positive influence, among Middle Class individuals without a college degree, exposure to Generalized Likes produces a marginally significant positive DiD shift of $\text{DiD} = +0.335$ ($SE = 0.183, t = 1.83, p = 0.0677$). All other conditions for this group remain statistically indistinguishable from zero ($p \ge 0.13$).

\subsection{Discrete Behavioral Choices: Inertia, Conformity, and Reactance}
While continuous change models identify average shifts across conditions, they mask non-linear behavioral choices where opposing individual movements cancel each other out in the aggregate. For instance, an average shift close to zero could reflect complete evaluative rigidity, or it could reflect equal proportions of respondents conforming and reacting in opposite directions. To examine these discrete underlying choices, we categorize responses into three mutually exclusive behaviors: \textit{Stay} ($\Delta = 0$, maintaining one's initial evaluation), \textit{Conform} (moving in the direction of others' evaluation), and \textit{React} (moving in the opposite direction of others' evaluation) and use this as the outcome variable in a multinomial logit model with the four status consistency categories as predictors.  

Figure \ref{fig:multinom} presents the Average Marginal Effects (AME) on the probability of each behavioral choice from an IPW-weighted multinomial logistic regression, compared against the reference group of Working Class respondents without a college degree. In the baseline reference group, the predicted probabilities are $73.8\%$ for staying, $14.2\%$ for conforming, and $12.0\%$ for reacting.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/Figure7_MultinomialBehavior.png}
    \caption{Average Marginal Effects on Behavioral Choices (Stay, Conform, React) Across Status-Consistency Groups (Age $\ge 24$ Primary Sample). Horizontal bars indicate differences in choice probability relative to the baseline reference category (Working Class, No College), with 95\% confidence intervals. Colors indicate statistical significance: red ($p < 0.05$), orange ($p < 0.10$), and grey ($p \ge 0.10$).}
    \label{fig:multinom}
\end{figure}

The marginal effects provide compelling empirical confirmation of \textit{Hypothesis 6 (Status Consistency)} and offer new insights into the social dynamics of status inconsistency. First, in terms of evaluative stability (Panel A: Stay), structural status consistency acts as the primary anchor of aesthetic confidence and inertia. Middle-class individuals with a college degree are significantly more likely to maintain their initial judgment than non-college workers ($\text{AME} = +0.073, SE = 0.013, z = 5.47, p < 0.001$), with an overall stay rate of $81.1\%$. In contrast, status-inconsistent individuals without a college degree exhibit lower stability ($\text{AME} = -0.026, SE = 0.014, z = -1.81, p = 0.070$), with their stay rate falling to $71.2\%$. College-educated workers also display attenuated stability ($\text{AME} = -0.012, p = 0.379$, stay rate $72.5\%$). This shows that evaluative inertia is not a simple linear function of high status; rather, both high-consistent (Middle/College) and low-consistent (Working/No College) individuals occupy unambiguous positions within the social structure that foster normative certainty, whereas status inconsistency breeds evaluative fluidity.

Second, in terms of conformity (Panel B), status inconsistency emerges as the primary driver of susceptibility to external social influence. Both status-inconsistent groups display large, highly significant increases in the probability of conforming to others' judgments. Working Class individuals with a college degree are $+4.2\%$ more likely to conform relative to the reference group ($\text{AME} = +0.042, SE = 0.012, z = 3.60, p = 0.0003$, overall conformity rate $18.4\%$), and Middle Class individuals without college are $+4.3\%$ more likely to conform ($\text{AME} = +0.043, SE = 0.012, z = 3.69, p = 0.0002$, conformity rate $18.5\%$). In stark contrast, high-status consistent individuals are significantly less likely to conform ($\text{AME} = -0.033, SE = 0.011, z = -3.08, p = 0.0021$, conformity rate $10.9\%$). This shows that when actors occupy contradictory locations in social space—possessing formal educational credentials while identifying as working class, or claiming middle-class status without institutional degrees—they experience evaluative cross-pressures and cognitive ambiguity that heighten their inclination to align with external social signals.

Finally, regarding oppositional reactance (Panel C), behavioral divergence is strongly structured by educational capital. Both college-educated groups are significantly less likely to move away from others' judgments compared to non-college workers: Working Class respondents with a college degree are $-3.0\%$ less likely to react ($\text{AME} = -0.030, SE = 0.010, z = -3.06, p = 0.0022$), and Middle Class college graduates are $-4.0\%$ less likely to react ($\text{AME} = -0.040, SE = 0.010, z = -4.20, p < 0.0001$). Meanwhile, middle-class individuals without a college degree exhibit a marginally lower reactance rate ($\text{AME} = -0.018, p = 0.0788$). This shows that oppositional reactance to external cues is concentrated among individuals without a college education, whereas institutionalized cultural capital inhibits oppositional defiance.

To confirm that these behavioral dynamics are driven specifically by status-laden social cues rather than by generic feedback about others' opinions, we re-estimated the multinomial model excluding the two Taste-Only conditions ($N = 1,643$). As displayed in Appendix Figure \ref{fig:sens_multinom}, the results are virtually identical: structural status consistency continues to anchor evaluative inertia (Middle Class with College: $+7.6\%, p < 0.001$), status inconsistency heightens conformity (Working Class with College: $+4.9\%, p < 0.001$; Middle Class without College: $+4.1\%, p < 0.001$), and oppositional reactance remains concentrated among non-college workers ($-3.1\%$ and $-4.1\%$ for college-educated groups, $p < 0.001$). Netting out generic popularity cues therefore confirms that status consistency uniquely structures discrete behavioral responses to social influence.

As summarized in Table \ref{tbl:hypotheses_summary}, the DiD framework clarifies which of our initial intuitions withstood experimental scrutiny in this study. First, we confirm \textit{Hypothesis 1 (Mere Exposure Effect)}: repeated aesthetic evaluation produces an intrinsic positive exposure drift in the absence of external cues ($\beta_1 = +0.156, p = 0.0044$), demonstrating that aesthetic appreciation naturally increases upon re-encountering a cultural object.

Second, testing generalized social influence reveals decisive evidence of \textit{valence asymmetry}. While \textit{Hypothesis 2a (Positive Evaluations)} is not supported because positive cues from generalized others fail to produce an increment significantly above baseline exposure drift ($\delta_{\text{Like}} = +0.047, p = 0.555$), \textit{Hypothesis 2b (Negative Evaluations)} is strongly supported ($\delta_{\text{Dislike}} = -0.186, p = 0.0169$). Learning that unknown others disliked the artwork completely overrides the mere exposure effect, confirming that negative consensus exerts a dependable veto on aesthetic appreciation ($\Delta = +0.233, p = 0.0035$).

Third, the status-stratified DiD framework shows that two prominent mechanisms---same-status alignment (\textit{Hypothesis 3}) and upward cultural goodwill (\textit{Hypothesis 4})---are not supported for status-consistent groups once counterfactual exposure drift is accounted for. For low-status consistent individuals, exposure to same-status likes ($\text{DiD} = -0.056, p = 0.690$) and high-status likes ($\text{DiD} = +0.097, p = 0.420$) remain statistically indistinguishable from zero. Instead, the primary status-driven dynamic is status-based distancing disalignment (\textit{Hypothesis 5}): high-status consistent respondents exposed to low-status likes exhibit a statistically significant negative divergence ($\text{DiD} = -0.308, p = 0.036$), providing clear experimental evidence of Bourdieusian symbolic distinction and taste abandonment.

Finally, discrete behavioral modeling provides robust confirmation of \textit{Hypothesis 6 (Status Consistency)}. Aesthetic stability (``staying'') is governed by structural status consistency rather than high status alone: both high-consistent and low-consistent individuals exhibit high inertia, whereas status-inconsistent individuals display heightened fluidity and conformity ($p < 0.001$).

\subsection{Limitations and Suggestions for Future Work}
Several limitations of this study suggest directions for future research. First, in terms of operationalization, our experiment relied on a single cultural stimulus (Whistler's \textit{Nocturne}), selected for its high baseline variance. Future studies should examine whether social influence dynamics operate similarly across abstract, figurative, and commercial cultural forms.

Regarding threats to causal inference and contextual confounding, while our design randomized information about others, respondents' status locations were observational. Although inverse probability weighting balanced observed demographic covariates, unmeasured psychological dispositions could contribute to status-group differences. Future research using laboratory status induction could provide complementary evidence.

Third, our study examined the unidirectional influence of aggregate fictional others on individuals. In everyday life, cultural influence operates bidirectionally within evolving social networks. Investigating how reciprocal feedback loops shape aesthetic consensus represents an important next step.

Fourth, regarding temporal granularity, our experiment measured short-term evaluative shifts during a survey session. Longitudinal studies tracking whether experimentally induced taste shifts persist over weeks or months would help determine the durability of these social influence effects.

Finally, regarding institutional scope conditions, our sample was recruited via a quota-matched online panel of U.S. adults. While providing greater demographic diversity than student subject pools, cross-national replications are needed to assess how national cultural institutions shape status distinction and conformity.

\subsection{Implications: Status Consistency and Evaluative Fluidity}
Our findings carry broad implications for sociological theories of taste and stratification. Classic cultural sociology has often framed aesthetic boundary work in vertical terms: high-status actors use exclusive tastes to mark distinction, while lower-status actors display cultural goodwill. Our results qualify this model by showing that cultural influence is bounded by valence asymmetry and structural consistency.

Most importantly, our analysis highlights status consistency as a neglected driver of aesthetic orientations. Status-consistent individuals—both high and low—display high evaluative stability, anchored in unambiguous structural locations. In contrast, status-inconsistent individuals experience normative cross-pressures that produce evaluative fluidity. By examining how structural consistency interacts with external social signals, cultural sociology can move beyond static homophily toward dynamic, interactional models of aesthetic judgment.

\section{Statistical Power and Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE) Analysis}
\label{sec:appendix_power_mde}

To rigorously evaluate the statistical precision and empirical detection boundaries of the study, we conducted a formal ex-post Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE) analysis \citep{bloom1995minimum}. Methodological scholarship has established that retrospective or ``post-hoc'' power calculations based on observed effect sizes and sample estimates are fundamentally flawed and uninformative, functioning merely as a tautological transformation of the observed $p$-value \citep{gelman2014beyond, hoenig2001abuse}. By contrast, an ex-post MDE analysis answers a principled design question: given the achieved cell sample sizes and the empirical variance structure of the repeated-measures data, what is the smallest true population effect size that the research design was adequately powered (at 80\% or 90\% power, two-tailed $\alpha = 0.05$) to detect?

In a repeated-measures Difference-in-Differences (DiD) framework where each respondent $i$ provides an initial pre-treatment aesthetic rating ($Y_{i1}$) and a post-treatment evaluation ($Y_{i2}$) on a 7-point Likert scale, the standard error of the change score $\Delta Y_i = Y_{i2} - Y_{i1}$ depends directly on the test-retest correlation $\rho$ between trials:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\Delta} = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2 \rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2}
\end{equation}
When the test-retest correlation is high, within-subject differencing cancels out stable individual evaluative variance, yielding a change-score standard deviation substantially smaller than the cross-sectional baseline standard deviation ($\sigma_{\Delta} \ll \sigma_1$). In our primary analytic sample, aesthetic judgments exhibit strong stability across repeated trials ($r = 0.8993$, with $\sigma_1 = 1.504$ and $\sigma_2 = 1.554$), yielding an empirical standard deviation of the change score, $\sigma_{\Delta} = 0.688$. This within-subject autocorrelation reduces the standard error of treatment comparisons by more than 50\% relative to an independent post-test design without pre-treatment baselines.

For a two-sample Difference-in-Differences comparison between a treatment condition of size $N_T$ and the unexposed Control Condition of size $N_C$, the standard error is:
\begin{equation}
SE_{\text{DiD}} = \sigma_{\Delta} \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_T} + \frac{1}{N_C}}
\end{equation}
The Minimum Detectable Effect at significance level $\alpha$ (two-tailed) and statistical power $1 - \beta$ is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
\text{MDE}_{\text{raw}} = (z_{1 - \alpha/2} + z_{1 - \beta}) \times SE_{\text{DiD}}
\end{equation}
where $z_{0.975} = 1.960$ for $\alpha = 0.05$, $z_{0.80} = 0.842$ for 80\% power (multiplier $= 2.802$), and $z_{0.90} = 1.282$ for 90\% power (multiplier $= 3.242$). To facilitate comparison across studies, effect sizes are expressed both in raw 7-point Likert scale units ($\text{MDE}_{\text{raw}}$) and as standardized effect sizes relative to baseline variance, Cohen's $d = \text{MDE}_{\text{raw}} / \sigma_1$ \citep{cohen1988statistical}.

The empirical findings from the MDE analysis yield three primary methodological takeaways. First, regarding repeated-measures exposure drift (Panel A of Table \ref{tbl:mde_analysis}), the full sample ($N = 2,253$) possessed extraordinary statistical power, capable of detecting shifts as minute as Cohen's $d \ge 0.028$ (0.041 scale points). Crucially, even within the unexposed Control Condition ($N_C = 177$), the design was powered at 80\% to detect a drift of $d \ge 0.099$ (0.148 scale points). This high baseline precision enabled us to reliably identify the empirical $+0.107$ exposure drift in the Control Condition ($t = 2.05, p = 0.041$), confirming that the upward drift was not an artifact of random noise.

Second, for the primary pooled Difference-in-Differences models compared against the unexposed Control Condition (Panel B of Table \ref{tbl:mde_analysis}), the design possessed 80\% power to detect small treatment effects: $d \ge 0.113$ (0.169 points) for pooled high-status conditions ($N_T = 575$), $d \ge 0.125$ (0.188 points) for single-status conditions ($N_T \approx 290$), and $d \ge 0.140$ (0.210 points) for taste-only conditions ($N_T = 172$). All three pooled thresholds fall comfortably below Cohen's standard threshold for a ``small'' effect size ($d = 0.20$). This confirms that the pooled models were well-equipped to detect subtle social influence cues.

Third, within the disaggregated status-consistency quadrants (Panel C of Table \ref{tbl:mde_analysis}), cell sizes are necessarily smaller, resulting in wider detection envelopes. In the two larger, status-consistent quadrants—Working Class without College ($N_C = 70$) and Middle Class with College ($N_C = 51$)—the median MDE thresholds were $d = 0.203$ (0.304 scale points) and $d = 0.231$ (0.347 scale points), providing adequate power for small-to-moderate effects. In contrast, in the smaller, status-inconsistent quadrants—Middle Class without College ($N_C = 33$) and Working Class with College ($N_C = 23$)—the median MDE thresholds increased to $d = 0.281$ (0.421 points) and $d = 0.348$ (0.522 points). Consequently, statistically significant shifts in these cells emerged only when behavioral adjustments were pronounced, such as the $-0.394$ negative divergence ($p < 0.001$) observed among college-educated working-class respondents exposed to High-Status Dislike cues. Methodologically, this indicates that non-significant coefficients in the smallest status cells should be interpreted as reflecting greater estimation uncertainty rather than definitive evidence of null effects.

\section{Survey Vignettes and Treatment Prompts}
\label{sec:appendix_vignettes}
Below we transcribe the verbatim wording, instructions, question stems, and response options of the survey instrument administered in the 2012 study, based on the original Qualtrics protocol.

\subsection*{1. Baseline Measurement (Trial 1, Survey Page 4)}
Respondents were presented with the high-resolution image of James McNeill Whistler's \textit{Nocturne: Blue and Gold -- Old Battersea Bridge} (1872) accompanied by the following verbatim introductory instruction and question prompt:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Screen Text / Instruction:} \\
\textit{``Please look at this painting for a few seconds.''} \\[4pt]
\textbf{Question Prompt:} \\
\textit{``Give us your first impression of this painting -- how much do you like it?''}
\end{quote}
Responses were recorded using the following 8 single-choice options:
\begin{itemize}
    \item 1 = I like it very much
    \item 2 = I like it
    \item 3 = I like it just a little
    \item 4 = I neither like it nor dislike it
    \item 5 = I dislike it just a little
    \item 6 = I dislike it
    \item 7 = I dislike it very much
    \item 8 = I can't see the image
\end{itemize}
Items were reverse-coded during data processing (1 = ``dislike very much'', 4 = ``neither like nor dislike'', 7 = ``like very much'') so that higher numerical values indicate greater aesthetic affinity. Respondents who selected ``I can't see the image'' ($N = 6$) were excluded from the analytic sample.

\subsection*{2. Experimental Treatment Vignettes (Survey Page 5)}
Following their initial evaluation, respondents in the experimental conditions were exposed to feedback regarding the evaluations of others. Notably, the prompt did \textit{not} describe the evaluating group in relative terms to the participant; instead, it presented absolute descriptive characteristics of the alter group, allowing social proximity or distance to emerge relationally from the participant's own social location.

\paragraph{A. Status-Differentiated Conditions (Class \& Taste):}
Respondents assigned to conditions crossing status and valence read:
\begin{quote}
\textit{``Thank you for your response. Our survey so far indicates that the group of people most likely to say that they [LIKE / DISLIKE] the painting you saw have completed\dots''}
\end{quote}
where the alter characteristics varied by experimental condition:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low-Status Others (LCC):} \textit{``\dots high school but did not go to college, and work in a food service job.''}
    \item \textbf{High-Status Economic Specialists (ES):} \textit{``\dots a Bachelor's degree in Business but no additional degrees, and work at a financial investment firm.''}
    \item \textbf{High-Status Cultural Specialists (CS):} \textit{``\dots several advanced degrees including a PhD, and work at a college or university.''}
\end{itemize}

Immediately following the vignette, respondents answered a subjective similarity probe:
\begin{quote}
\textit{``Given only what you know about this group's education, occupation, and opinion about the painting, how similar do you think you are to the people in this group?''}
\end{quote}
with response options: \textit{Very similar}, \textit{Somewhat similar}, \textit{Just a little similar}, \textit{Not very similar}, or \textit{Not at all similar}.

\paragraph{B. Undifferentiated Taste-Only Conditions (Control 2):}
Respondents in the Taste-Only conditions were told:
\begin{quote}
\textit{``Thank you for your response. Other people who have taken this survey recently have said that they [LIKE / DISLIKE] the painting you saw.''} \\[4pt]
\textit{``Given only what you know about these people's opinion about the painting, how similar do you think you are to them?''}
\end{quote}
with the identical 5-point similarity response options.

\paragraph{C. Unexposed Control Condition (No Alter):}
Respondents assigned to the pure Control Condition received no feedback screen, evaluation valence, or information about others whatsoever, proceeding directly from Trial 1 to the intervening survey block.

\subsection*{3. Alter Attribution Probe (Survey Page 6, Treatment Respondents Only)}
Immediately after the similarity question, respondents in the status-differentiated conditions completed an attribution check:
\begin{quote}
\textit{``In the last question, when you thought about how similar you are to the people in this group, which factor was most influential?''}
\end{quote}
with response options:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Their education and/or occupation
    \item Their opinion about the painting
    \item A combination of both
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{4. Intervening Survey Battery (Cognitive Buffer, Survey Pages 7--15)}
Between Trial 1 and Trial 2, all respondents completed substantive survey modules lasting 15 to 20 minutes, comprising full educational history (highest degree attained and college major field), intergenerational educational capital (parental bachelor's degree attainment), employment status, detailed 22-category Standard Occupational Classification (SOC), annual personal pre-tax income (23 income brackets), subjective social class self-identification (Lower class, Working class, Middle class, Upper class), and an extensive musical taste battery assessing liking, listening frequency, favorite genre, and perceived audience demographic stereotypes across 20 distinct musical genres.

\subsection*{5. Post-Treatment Measurement (Trial 2, Survey Page 16)}
Following the intervening modules, respondents were re-shown the identical artwork and asked:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Screen Text / Instruction:} \\
\textit{``Please look at this painting for a few seconds again.''} \\[4pt]
\textbf{Question Prompt:} \\
\textit{``Give us your final impression of this painting -- how much do you like it?''}
\end{quote}
recorded using the identical 8 single-choice options (from 1 = ``I like it very much'' to 7 = ``I dislike it very much'', plus ``I can't see the image''), reverse-coded on the same 1--7 scale.

\section{Sensitivity Analysis: Behavioral Choices Excluding Taste-Only Conditions}
\label{sec:appendix_sens_multinom}
To verify that the discrete behavioral patterns documented in the main text (Figure \ref{fig:multinom}) are specifically generated by status-differentiated social influence rather than by undifferentiated taste-consensus cues, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding respondents assigned to the two Taste-Only conditions ($N = 300$ in the primary sample). The resulting analytic sample ($N = 1,643$) restricts behavioral classification to respondents exposed to feedback that combines evaluation valence and others' occupational/educational status, alongside unexposed control stayers.

Figure \ref{fig:sens_multinom} displays the Average Marginal Effects from this sensitivity specification, compared against Working Class respondents without a college degree. As shown in the figure, all substantive patterns persist with exceptional stability: status-consistent college-educated respondents exhibit elevated evaluative stability ($\text{AME} = +0.076, p < 0.001$), status-inconsistent respondents exhibit heightened conformity ($\text{AME} = +0.049, p < 0.001$ for Working Class with College; $\text{AME} = +0.041, p < 0.001$ for Middle Class without College), and college education sharply reduces oppositional reactance ($\text{AME} = -0.031, p < 0.001$ and $\text{AME} = -0.041, p < 0.001$). Notably, under this status-restricted specification, the lower evaluative stability of status-inconsistent non-college respondents remains apparent, reinforcing our core finding that status crystallization anchors aesthetic judgments against external social influence.

\section{Robustness Check: Alternative Definition of Social Class}
\label{sec:appendix_class_robustness}
Restricting the subjective class definition strictly to respondents self-identifying as ``working class'' or ``middle class'' (excluding lower and upper class extremes) produces identical conclusions: status-consistent groups maintain the highest stay rates (Middle/College: 80.5\%; Working/No College: 74.1\%) and status-inconsistent groups maintain the highest conformity rates (Middle/No College: 18.2\%; Working/College: 17.6\%).

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:did_edu_4tiers} and Table \ref{tbl:did_edu_4tiers}, disaggregating education reveals a monotonic, stepped cultural capital gradient that substantively enriches the main findings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Tier 1 (High School or Less):} Respondents with a high school education or less are the only group in the study that exhibits positive evaluative shifts, showing positive facilitation under Generalized Likes ($\text{DiD} = +0.284, p = 0.061$) and an oppositional positive reaction when informed that low-status others disliked the artwork ($\text{DiD} = +0.240, p = 0.074$). They are completely indifferent to high-status opinions.
    \item \textbf{Tier 2 (Two-Year Associate Degree / Some College):} Respondents occupying the intermediate sub-baccalaureate tier display complete evaluative stability: all six treatment estimates hover near zero (ranging from $-0.11$ to $+0.14$), with no condition approaching statistical significance ($p \ge 0.25$). Ancillary tests confirm that terminal Associate degree holders ($N = 196$) and those with some college but no degree ($N = 459$) are equally inert.
    \item \textbf{Tier 3 (Bachelor's Degree):} Upon crossing the 4-year baccalaureate threshold, evaluative behavior flips decisively into selective downward correction: Bachelor's degree holders adjust downward under High-Status Dislike ($\text{DiD} = -0.271, p = 0.030$) and Generalized Dislike ($\text{DiD} = -0.308, p = 0.046$), while ignoring all positive endorsements ($p \ge 0.75$).
    \item \textbf{Tier 4 (Postgraduate Degree):} Among respondents holding advanced credentials (Master's, Doctoral, and Professional degrees), cultural boundary-making and taste abandonment emerge in their most intense form. Exposure to Low-Status Likes produces a massive downward divergence of $\text{DiD} = -0.491$ ($SE = 0.135, t = -3.64, p = 0.0003$), representing the single largest negative treatment effect observed in the experiment. Postgraduates also exhibit pervasive sensitivity to dislikes across all categories (High-Status, Low-Status, and Generalized Dislikes, all $p < 0.03$).
\end{enumerate}

These patterns confirm that collapsing Tiers 1 and 2 into ``No College'' and Tiers 3 and 4 into ``College Degree'' accurately reflects the primary institutional fault line of cultural capital, while disaggregation demonstrates that Bourdieusian distinction intensifies monotonically with advanced educational attainment.
